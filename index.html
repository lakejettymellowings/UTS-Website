01. the claim

Achievement isn't supposed to be your whole identity.

nobody ever said this out loud

A grade, a rank, a comparison. Somewhere along the way it stopped being feedback on one piece of work and started feeling like a fact about you. It got absorbed from a hundred small moments, which is why it's so hard to argue with.

Two things tend to get tangled together here. Self-imposed pressure is the push you put on yourself, often long after anyone else stopped asking. Maladaptive perfectionism is when that push hardens into needing everything flawless and treating anything less as failure. They often travel together and feed each other. Neither is a diagnosis, and this page does not screen for either one.

Once a standard has a source, it can be checked. Most don't hold up.

02. the self-check

Notice which one it feels like.

no wrong answers, promise

Nothing clinical, nothing scored. Just five moments. Answer for how you actually react, not how you think you should.

04. the limits

What this can't do.

so nobody expects a miracle

  • Only reaches people who look

    Nothing here shows up uninvited, the way a feed post would.

  • One visit won't undo years

    It doesn't touch grading culture, school stress or family expectations. Those sit outside anything a website can fix.

  • Those who need it may skip it

    This kind of pressure often comes with not wanting to admit the message applies to you.

  • Easy to forget

    Without a school link, a counselor mention or a friend sharing it, a single visit is gone once the tab closes.

Research on peer acceptance suggests it can directly ease perfectionistic tendencies in teens (Blackburn et al., 2025). That is part of why routing this through schools or counselors, rather than leaving it to be found cold, matters more than the site itself.

Who Told You That? is a short, anonymous page where students look at self-imposed pressure and maladaptive perfectionism, trace a rule they hold themselves to back to where it came from, then rewrite it in a kinder way. It asks for no name or account and works on a phone. It is a starting point for a conversation, not a screening tool and not a replacement for counseling.

  1. Put it where students already look. A QR code on a classroom wall, a counselor's door or a group chat link.
  2. Let them read it alone. Nothing is graded, and posting to the wall is optional and anonymous.
  3. Ask the question out loud. "Who told you that?" works well as a check-in prompt afterward.

some important terms

Tap a word to open it.

Standard

A bar or rule for what counts as good enough, like the grade you should get or how much rest you are allowed. On this site it mostly means the unwritten ones nobody ever said out loud.

High standards

Wanting to do good work and being okay when you fall short. It aims at doing well, not at being flawless.

Not the same as perfectionism.

Perfectionism

Holding yourself to a very high bar and being hard on yourself when you miss it. It can push you forward or wear you down, depending on how it treats mistakes.

Maladaptive perfectionism

The kind that costs you more than it gives. It shows up as a big fear of mistakes, harsh self-criticism and putting things off.

A pattern, not a diagnosis, and not the same as caring about quality.

Self-imposed pressure

Pressure that comes from inside your own head. “Self” does not mean it started with you or that it is your fault, since many of these standards were picked up from other people.

Internalized standard

A rule you absorbed from family, school or your feeds until it started to feel like your own opinion.

Fused to your worth

When a standard and your sense of value become one thing in your head, so missing the standard feels like being worth less.

Self-worth

The sense that you matter no matter how a grade, a result or a post turns out.

Feeling found out

The fear that a mistake or a lower grade proves you were never as capable as people thought. It is often called the impostor feeling.

It is a feeling, not a fact.

Reframe

A kinder rewrite of a harsh thought that is still honest. It does not pretend nothing went wrong.

Peer acceptance

Feeling welcome with classmates and friends as you are, not only when you do well. The research the site points to links it with easing perfectionistic tendencies in teens.

Grading culture

A school setting where grades and rank become the main way students are measured and compared. The site says this sits outside what a website can fix.

Source

On the wall, where you think a standard came from, like a person, a place or a feed. You can leave it blank or say you are still tracing it.

Word cloud

The picture on the wall of where standards come from. The bigger a word, the more people mention it, and the color shows the kind of source.

Leans

On the self check, which way your answers tilted today. It is a tendency, not a score, a label or a diagnosis.
