Who told you that?

Most of the standards you're failing to meet were never yours to begin with. This traces a few of them back to where they came from.

01 — the claim

Achievement isn't supposed to be your whole identity.

Somewhere along the way, a grade, a rank, or a comparison got treated as a fact about who you are, instead of one piece of feedback on one piece of work. Nobody sat you down and told you that directly. It got absorbed from a hundred smaller moments: a look, a comment, a silence after a bad result. That's exactly why it's hard to argue with. It was never stated clearly enough to be questioned.

Being specific about where a standard came from is usually the first crack in it. A teacher's offhand comment in 2019 is not a permanent verdict. A parent's own unresolved fear about money or status is not a fact about your worth. Once a claim has a source, it can be checked. Most of these don't hold up under a check.

02 — the self-check

Notice which one it feels like.

Nothing clinical, nothing scored. Just three moments. Answer for how you actually tend to react, not how you think you should.

1 / 3

You get back a grade or a result that's worse than you expected.

2 / 3

Someone points out a mistake in something you made.

3 / 3

You imagine turning in work that's genuinely just "good enough."

leans: try again

Setbacks mostly feel like information for you, not a verdict. That's worth keeping. It's the opposite of the pattern this project is about.

leans: exposed

If falling short feels less like "I can improve" and more like "I've been found out," that's usually the sign worth sitting with. Not because something's wrong with you, but because a standard got fused to your worth somewhere along the way.

04 — the limits

What this can't do.

  • It only reaches people who go looking for it. Nothing here shows up uninvited, the way a feed post would.
  • It can't undo years of internalized pressure in one visit, and it doesn't touch grading culture, school stress, or family expectations directly. Those exist outside anything a website can fix.
  • The people who'd benefit most are often the least likely to look, since perfectionistic tendencies tend to come with not wanting to admit the message applies to you.
  • Without something pulling people back, such as a school link, a counselor mention, or a friend sharing it, a single visit is easy to forget once the tab closes.

Research on peer acceptance suggests it can directly ease perfectionistic tendencies in teens (Blackburn et al., 2025). That is part of why routing this through schools or counselors, rather than leaving it to be found cold, matters more than the site itself.

05 — a way out

Rewrite the line.

Once you can see where a rule came from, you get to edit it. A few from the top of the page.

I'll earn rest once I'm caught up.Rest is part of the work.
One mistake and people will think less of me.One mistake is just one mistake.
Good enough isn't good enough.Good enough is allowed to be enough.
Think of one standard you hold yourself to. Who told you that? Then ask what you'd say to a friend who held the same one.
